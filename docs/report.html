Phase 1

Deep comprehension and the Math Bridge

What the file actually contains

flood_final.qmd has three R chunks that do different jobs, and it is worth being precise about them in an interview.

  1. Chunk 1 is Finn Lindgren's SPDE tutorial (isbaspde.R, referenced in your notes): a simulated Matérn field on the unit square with two replicates. It is a correctness harness, not your analysis. Say so; it shows you validated the machinery on a known truth before touching data, which is exactly what a simulator-first lab team wants to hear.
  2. Chunk 2 is the real model. It reads Processed_Final_data.csv, subsamples 3 000 rows, jitters the coordinates, builds an inla.mesh.2d mesh (cutoff 2°, max edge 5 to 6°), and fits a Gaussian-likelihood SPDE model with empirical-Bayes hyperparameters.
  3. Chunk 3 is diagnostics. Posterior mean versus observed, residual histogram, a residual map, and a plot of absolute error against posterior standard deviation. That last plot is an uncertainty-calibration check, and it becomes the single most important figure in this whole story.

Inputs, outputs, statistical logic

Data
4 577 monthly rows, 13 Pacific tide gauges (Fiji, Kiribati, Tonga, Vanuatu, Samoa, Tuvalu, Nauru, Cook Islands, Marshall Islands, Solomon Islands, Papua New Guinea, Micronesia, Niue), October 1992 to 2025. Columns: Month, Year, Gaps, Good, Minimum, Maximum, Mean, Std_Dev, Location, Latitude, Longitude.
Response
y = Mean, monthly mean sea level in metres relative to each gauge's own datum.
Fixed effects
An explicit intercept and covar = Std_Dev, the within-month variability (essentially tidal range).
Latent field
f(field, model = spde) with alpha = 2, a Matérn field with smoothness \(\nu = \alpha - d/2 = 1\), represented as a Gaussian Markov random field on the mesh through the SPDE \((\kappa^2 - \Delta)^{\alpha/2}(\tau u) = \mathcal W\).
Inference
INLA: nested Laplace approximations for the latent Gaussian field, strategy = "gaussian" for the marginals, int.strategy = "eb" so the hyperparameters \((\log\tau, \log\kappa)\) are fixed at their posterior mode rather than integrated out. That is type-II maximum likelihood, empirical Bayes.
Outputs
Posterior mean and sd of the fitted values at the 3 000 points (the two maps), their histograms, residuals, and in chunk 1 the posterior marginal of the practical range.

Where a principal engineer would push, and why that helps you

Only 13 distinct locations exist. The 3 000 "samples" are the same 13 coordinates repeated about 230 times each with a 10⁻⁵ jitter, and a mesh cutoff of 2° merges them back onto 13 nodes. The spatial field is therefore a 13-level random effect, and the ~390 monthly values per station are treated as iid noise around a station mean. Time, which the older flood project.qmd handled with an RW2 model, is absent here.

The response is datum-relative. Fiji's 1.28 m versus Solomon Islands' 0.71 m is a gauge-datum offset, not oceanography. The comparable physical quantity is the rise rate; when I recompute it from your data it is 1.5 to 9.8 mm/yr, with Samoa's 9.8 reflecting post-2009 earthquake subsidence.

The evaluation is in-sample. Posterior-mean-versus-observed on training points measures fit, not prediction.

Hygiene. The CSV has string-typed numerics, 23 duplicate station-months, and a row with Year = 239890.

None of that is a mark against you. Each item is a seam where the AI4S version of the project starts: the twin below uses rise rate as the target property, separates prior knowledge (the 13 gauges) from new experiments, evaluates out of sample against a hidden truth, and treats the space-time structure as the state of a sequential decision problem.

Why a statistics background is an unfair advantage here. The three ways self-driving labs fail in practice are surrogate collapse (hyperparameters running to a degenerate optimum), over-confident acquisition (uncalibrated posterior sd), and wasted budget (no design-of-experiments logic). Those are, respectively, identifiability, calibration, and optimal design, which is the core syllabus of a Master of Statistics. An ML engineer learns them by burning reagents; you arrive knowing them.

Phase 2

The RL and closed-loop AI4S paradigm

The reframing is concrete: the tide-gauge network is a laboratory whose experiments are expensive site deployments, and your INLA model is its analysis pipeline. Everything an autonomous lab needs sits on top of that pair.

Environment: SeaLevelLab-v0 (Gymnasium)

The simulator is a digital twin. Its hidden truth is one posterior draw of a Matérn GP fitted to the 13 real gauges' rise rates, so it honours the data at the stations and has unknown structure between them, like the real ocean. Fitted hyperparameters from your CSV: length-scale 10.7° of longitude (practical range 0.305 of the box), signal sd 1.72 mm/yr, nugget 1.106 mm/yr, mean 5.162 mm/yr. A physical experiment is one call to measure(x): deploy a gauge or run a campaign at location \(x\) and read back a noisy rise rate.

class DigitalTwin:
    """Ground truth = one posterior draw of the station-fitted GP (so it honours the 13 real
    gauges but has unknown structure between them, like the real ocean).  Each `reset` of the
    Gymnasium env draws a new truth -> the RL policy must generalise, not memorise.

    `measure(x)` is the *physical experiment*: it is the only channel through which an agent
    learns about the truth, and each call costs budget.  [HKQAI JD: coordinate physical and
    computational experiments -- the twin is the physical side, the GP belief is the
    computational side]
    """

    def reset(self, *, seed: int | None = None, options: dict | None = None):
        super().reset(seed=seed)
        self.twin.sample_truth(self.np_random)            # a NEW hidden truth every episode -> policies must generalise
        self.belief = MaternGP(self.twin.hyper)           # the lab's analysis pipeline (= the INLA model, ported)
        if self.warm_start:
            self.belief.set_data(self.twin.X_st, self.twin.y_st)
        self.X_run, self.y_run, self.history, self.t = [], [], [], 0
        mu, sd = self.belief.posterior(self.grid)
        self.ipv0 = self.ipv = float((sd**2).mean())
        self.best_y, self.best_x = float(self.twin.hyper.mean), None   # prior expectation is the baseline to beat
        return self._obs(mu, sd), self._info(mu, sd)

    def step(self, action):
        x = np.clip(np.asarray(action, dtype=float).reshape(2), 0.0, 1.0)
        y = self.twin.measure(x, self.np_random)          # (1) PHYSICAL experiment  [JD: coordinate physical experiments]
        self.belief.add(x, y)                             # (2) COMPUTATIONAL update  [JD: coordinate computational experiments]
        self.X_run.append(x); self.y_run.append(y); self.t += 1
        mu, sd = self.belief.posterior(self.grid)
        ipv_new = float((sd**2).mean())
        if self.mode == "active_learning":
            reward = (self.ipv - ipv_new) / self.ipv0 - self.cost
        else:
            reward = max(0.0, y - self.best_y) / self.twin.hyper.signal_sd - self.cost
        self.ipv = ipv_new
        if y > self.best_y:
            self.best_y, self.best_x = y, x
        terminated = self.t >= self.budget
        info = self._info(mu, sd)
        info.update(x=x, y=float(y), x_deg=to_degrees(x)[0].round(2).tolist(), reward=float(reward))
        self.history.append(info)
        return self._obs(mu, sd), float(reward), terminated, False, info

Two scientific questions, one environment

  • Active learning, warm start. The 13 gauges are prior data. "Where should the fourteenth gauge go?" is optimal sensor placement.
  • Optimisation, cold start. The stations only inform the prior; a fresh campaign must find the highest-rise-rate site. That is hot-spot search, and it is the shape of every "find the best composition" problem in materials.

How Bayesian optimisation closes the loop

flowchart LR
  A["Belief: GP posterior (mu, sigma) on grid + budget"] --> B["Acquisition: qNegIntegratedPosteriorVariance or qLogNoisyEI"]
  B --> C["optimize_acqf -> next (lon, lat)"]
  C --> D["Physical experiment: twin.measure(x) + noise"]
  D --> E["Computational update: refit SingleTaskGP (fit_gpytorch_mll)"]
  E --> A
  E --> R["Reward: IPV reduction or improvement"]

Each iteration refits the surrogate on everything measured so far, maximises an acquisition function over the design space, and hands the argmax to env.step. The surrogate is the same Matérn GP as your SPDE field, refit every step instead of once. Two choices matter for a real instrument: the priors that stop the refit from collapsing (see the Math Bridge), and noisy expected improvement, which integrates over the uncertainty of the "best observed" value instead of trusting a lucky draw.

def fit_surrogate(X: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray) -> SingleTaskGP:
    """Matern-3/2 GP with ARD -- separate east-west / north-south ranges, an anisotropy the
    isotropic INLA SPDE in the R code could not express.

    The log-normal priors are not decoration: with ~15-30 noisy points the marginal likelihood
    happily collapses to "length-scale 0.3 deg, noise 0" (pure interpolation).  R-INLA guards
    against this with PC priors on (range, sigma); here the same guard is MAP-II with LogNormal
    priors, so fit_gpytorch_mll maximises log-likelihood + log-prior.  A statistician recognises
    this instantly; many ML engineers learn it the hard way.
    """
    train_X = torch.as_tensor(np.asarray(X, float), dtype=DT)
    train_Y = torch.as_tensor(np.asarray(y, float), dtype=DT).reshape(-1, 1)
    covar = ScaleKernel(MaternKernel(nu=1.5, ard_num_dims=2, lengthscale_prior=LogNormalPrior(np.log(0.25), 0.5)),
                        outputscale_prior=LogNormalPrior(0.0, 1.0))
    lik = GaussianLikelihood(noise_prior=LogNormalPrior(-2.0, 1.0), noise_constraint=GreaterThan(1e-3))
    model = SingleTaskGP(train_X, train_Y, covar_module=covar, likelihood=lik, outcome_transform=Standardize(m=1))
    fit_gpytorch_mll(ExactMarginalLogLikelihood(model.likelihood, model))   # type-II MAP == INLA "eb" + PC priors
    return model
def propose(model: SingleTaskGP, mode: str, mc_points: torch.Tensor, best_f: float) -> np.ndarray:
    if mode == "active_learning":
        # a-optimal design: pick x minimising the *integrated* posterior variance over the domain
        acq = qNegIntegratedPosteriorVariance(model, mc_points=mc_points)
    else:
        # measurements are noisy (gauge noise ~0.6 mm/yr), so the "best observed" is itself uncertain:
        # noisy-EI integrates over that instead of trusting a lucky draw (Letham et al. 2019)
        acq = qLogNoisyExpectedImprovement(model, X_baseline=model.train_inputs[0], prune_baseline=True)
    cand, _ = optimize_acqf(acq, bounds=BOUNDS, q=1, num_restarts=8, raw_samples=128)
    return cand.detach().numpy().reshape(2)


def bo_steps(env: SeaLevelLabEnv, n_steps: int, mode: str | None = None, n_init: int = 3, verbose: bool = False) -> list[dict]:
    """Continue the CURRENT episode for n_steps with BoTorch.  Called by the LLM agent as a tool,
    so the objective (`mode`) may be switched mid-episode: same lab, same belief, new goal."""
    if mode is not None:
        env.mode = mode
    mc_points = torch.as_tensor(env.grid, dtype=DT)
    sobol = torch.quasirandom.SobolEngine(2, scramble=True, seed=int(env.np_random.integers(1 << 30)))
    out = []
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        if env.t >= env.budget:
            break
        X, y = env.observed_data()
        if len(y) < n_init:                                   # cold start -> space-filling design
            x = sobol.draw(1, dtype=DT).numpy().reshape(2)
        else:
            model = fit_surrogate(X, y)
            x = propose(model, env.mode, mc_points, best_f=float(np.max(y)))
        obs, r, term, trunc, info = env.step(x)
        out.append(info)
        if verbose:
            print(f"  BO t={info['n_experiments']:2d} x={info['x_deg']} y={info['y']:.2f} r={r:+.3f} IPV={info['ipv_fraction']:.3f} regret={info['simple_regret']:.2f}")
    return out

Where reinforcement learning fits

Bayesian optimisation is a myopic planner: it refits and maximises a one-step acquisition every time. A PPO policy trained across many episodes with different hidden truths learns an amortised acquisition function \(\pi(a\mid \text{belief})\), including non-myopic behaviour such as spreading early experiments and exploiting late ones. Both consume the same belief vector, so the lab can swap them without touching hardware code. In the benchmark below the PPO policy, after 40 000 training steps, still leaves 0.92 of the map variance against 0.79 for random placement: it has not yet learned to spread its experiments. That is stated rather than tuned away. Amortised experimental design needs orders of magnitude more episodes and a convolutional encoder of the belief map, which is why production labs run BO as the default and keep RL as the research track.

def _symmetric(env: SeaLevelLabEnv):
    """SB3 gotcha: its Gaussian policy starts centred on 0 and clips to the action box, so with a
    [0,1] box half the early proposals pile up on the corner (140E, 25S).  A symmetric [-1,1] box,
    rescaled back to the lab's [0,1] design space by the wrapper, removes that bias."""
    return RescaleAction(env, min_action=-1.0, max_action=1.0)


def train_ppo(twin: DigitalTwin, mode: str, budget: int = 15, total_timesteps: int = 15_000, seed: int = 0, verbose: int = 0) -> PPO:
    env = Monitor(_symmetric(SeaLevelLabEnv(twin, mode=mode, budget=budget)))
    model = PPO(
        "MlpPolicy", env,
        n_steps=budget * 16, batch_size=budget * 4, n_epochs=10, learning_rate=3e-4,
        gamma=1.0,                      # finite horizon, budget is in the state -> undiscounted return is the natural objective
        ent_coef=0.01, seed=seed, verbose=verbose,
        policy_kwargs=dict(net_arch=[128, 128]),
    )
    model.learn(total_timesteps=total_timesteps)
    return model

Agentic workflow and tool calling

Design rules that survive contact with a real lab

  • Tools return statistics, not arrays. The model reads IPV fraction, the least-known cell and its sd, the best measurement, never 288 floats. This keeps reasoning grounded and tokens cheap.
  • Tools are budget-aware and fail softly. A request that would overspend returns an error string the model can read and re-plan around, instead of raising.
  • One audited path to hardware. The LLM cannot call the instrument; it calls run_experiment or run_bo_campaign, both of which go through env.step, where budget, logging and safety limits live.
  • Provider-agnostic schema. A minimal @tool decorator derives the JSON schema from the signature and docstring, the same shape LangChain, OpenAI and Anthropic all consume. The live path uses the Anthropic SDK's @beta_tool on the very same functions.
def tool(fn: Callable) -> Callable:
    """Minimal `@tool`: derives the JSON schema Anthropic / LangChain / OpenAI all expect from the
    signature + docstring.  Same shape as `anthropic.beta_tool`, so the toolbox is provider-agnostic."""
    sig = inspect.signature(fn, eval_str=True)          # eval_str: resolve `from __future__ import annotations`
    props, required = {}, []
    for name, p in sig.parameters.items():
        ann = p.annotation if p.annotation is not inspect._empty else str
        props[name] = {"type": _PY2JSON.get(ann, "string")}
        if p.default is inspect._empty:
            required.append(name)
    fn.tool_spec = {"name": fn.__name__, "description": inspect.getdoc(fn) or "",
                    "input_schema": {"type": "object", "properties": props, "required": required}}
    return fn

Three things in that trace are the "reasoning" an interviewer is asking about. The agent switches objective when the gain per experiment drops below 1.2 % of prior variance, which is a stopping rule, not a fixed schedule. It reads the predicted peak and its sd before exploiting, so it is exploiting belief, not a single measurement. And it refits the surrogate at the end and compares the length-scale with the twin's prior, which is a statistician's check that the hot-spot is basin-scale physics and not a local artefact.

The live version

With credentials present, the same toolbox is handed to Claude Opus 5 through the SDK's tool runner, which executes the tools and loops until the model stops calling them. Server-side fallbacks are enabled so a refusal is routed rather than crashing an experiment queue. Multi-agent extensions follow naturally: a planner agent, an experimentalist that owns the BO tool, and a critic that owns the calibration check, which is the AutoGen pattern with the statistician's role made explicit.

class ClaudeScientistAgent:
    """Anthropic SDK tool runner around the same toolbox.  Requires credentials
    (ANTHROPIC_API_KEY or `ant auth login`).  Model: claude-opus-5 with server-side fallbacks."""

    def __init__(self, toolbox: LabToolbox, model: str = "claude-opus-5"):
        import anthropic
        from anthropic import beta_tool
        self.client = anthropic.Anthropic()
        self.model = model
        self.tools = [beta_tool(f) for f in toolbox.functions]      # schema generated from signature + docstring

    def run(self, goal: str) -> list[str]:
        trace = []
        runner = self.client.beta.messages.tool_runner(
            model=self.model, max_tokens=16000, system=SYSTEM_PROMPT, tools=self.tools,
            messages=[{"role": "user", "content": goal}],
            betas=["server-side-fallback-2026-07-01"], fallbacks="default",
        )
        for message in runner:                                       # SDK executes tools and loops until end_turn
            for block in message.content:
                if block.type == "text":
                    trace.append(f"Claude:      {block.text}"); print(trace[-1])
                elif block.type == "tool_use":
                    trace.append(f"Action:      {block.name}({json.dumps(block.input)})"); print(trace[-1])
        return trace

Read the numbers the way a reviewer would. Active learning is where the surrogate's uncertainty is used directly: BoTorch removes 32 % of the prior map variance against 21 % for random sampling, with a spread across truths several times smaller. Optimisation with a cold start and 15 noisy experiments in a box six length-scales wide is a hard problem, and the regret numbers overlap; that is the honest result, and it is why the agent in Phase 3 spends budget on characterisation first. PPO is below random in both modes (0.92 variance remaining; regret 2.60 mm/yr). Two engineering details already went into it, a symmetric action wrapper so the untrained Gaussian policy is not clipped into one corner of the domain, and an undiscounted return because the budget is part of the state; what it still lacks is training scale and a belief encoder. Reporting it this way is deliberate: an interviewer will trust the BoTorch numbers more because the RL numbers were not hidden.

The hyperparameter-prior lesson, in code

# Weakly-informative priors on log-hyper-parameters.  With only 13 gauges the marginal
# likelihood cannot separate "short range + big nugget" from "long range + small nugget"
# (range/sigma are only jointly identifiable: Zhang 2004).  R-INLA solves this with PC priors
# on (range, sigma) -- the `theta.prior.mean / theta.prior.prec` lines in flood_final.qmd.
# We do the same with log-normal priors, i.e. MAP-II instead of ML-II.  Basin-scale sea-level
# trend patterns (ENSO / PDO) are coherent over ~15-30 deg of longitude, hence the prior mode
# of 0.25 (unit box) = 17.5 deg.  Samoa's 9.8 mm/yr post-2009-earthquake subsidence is local
# tectonics, which the nugget absorbs instead of bending the whole field.
DEFAULT_PRIORS = dict(log_lengthscale=(np.log(0.25), 0.5), log_noise_sd=(np.log(0.8), 0.5))
Phase 5

Interview pitch and the so-what

Previously, I built a Bayesian spatial model of Pacific sea level in R-INLA. Thirteen tide gauges, thirty years of monthly data, a Matérn field represented through the SPDE approach, empirical-Bayes hyperparameters, and posterior mean and uncertainty maps with a calibration check of the posterior standard deviation against actual error. It answered a static question: what does the field look like given the data we already have.

I upgraded it into the intelligence layer of a self-driving laboratory. The insight is that my SPDE field is mathematically a Gaussian-process surrogate, so I ported it to Python, turned it into a digital twin that generates hidden ground truths, and wrapped the network as a Gymnasium environment where the state is the GP belief, the action is the next experiment location, and the reward is either information gain or improvement of the target property. On top of that I closed the loop three ways: BoTorch with integrated-posterior-variance and noisy expected-improvement acquisitions, Optuna as a second driver, and a PPO policy from Stable-Baselines3 that learns an amortised acquisition function. Finally I put an LLM agent above the loop that reasons over compact statistics, calls the lab as tools, decides when to switch from characterising to exploiting, and checks the surrogate's physics before reporting.

This solves the exact problem HKQAI has: a lab that must choose expensive experiments under uncertainty, coordinate a physical instrument with a computational model, and be driven by an agent that can explain itself. In the benchmark the closed loop removes 32 percent of the map uncertainty for the same budget that random experimentation spends to remove 21, and the agent's transcript is a readable plan-experiment-interpret record. The part I would stress is what my statistics training caught: with few data points the surrogate's range and noise are not separately identifiable, and the maximum-likelihood refit collapsed until I added the priors INLA taught me to use. That failure mode wastes reagents in a real lab, and I know how to prevent it.

Likely follow-up questions and short answers

Why not just use Optuna everywhere?
Optuna treats the objective as a black box. Active learning needs the posterior variance inside the surrogate, so it needs a modelling library like BoTorch. Optuna is kept as a second driver for the optimisation objective to show the loop is not tied to one tool.
Why is PPO worse than BoTorch?
Fifteen-step episodes, a 290-dimensional belief, and 40 000 training steps. Amortised policies need far more episodes and usually a learned encoder of the belief map. The value is a policy that is cheap at inference and can be non-myopic; the benchmark states where it currently stands rather than hiding it.
How would this touch a real instrument?
Replace twin.measure with a driver call. Nothing above env.step changes, which is the point of the Gymnasium boundary: agents are tested on the twin and promoted to hardware.
What about the temporal dimension you dropped?
Add time as a third action coordinate and an AR(1) ⊗ Matérn separable kernel, the space-time SPDE. The belief-MDP construction is unchanged.
What is the risk with the LLM?
Hallucinated reasoning, so the LLM never sees raw hardware and every tool validates its arguments and budget. The reasoning is logged as a transcript, and the critic role is a statistician's calibration check.
